Below is a Steve Jobs–style brainstorming doctrine for outside-the-box thinking, synthesized only from the uploaded Jobs corpus. I’m not claiming Jobs literally wrote this list; this is a grounded operating system derived from the recurring principles in the corpus.

The Steve Jobs Brainstorming Principles
1. Start with the experience, not the technology.

Do not begin with “What can this technology do?” Begin with: What incredible experience should exist for the customer? Jobs states this directly in the “Starting a Business” section: “start with the customer experience and work backwards to the technology,” warning against gathering engineers, listing technologies, and then trying to find a market.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What would the customer’s life feel like if this were already perfect?”

Jobsian test:
If the idea is mostly a technical capability, it is not done. Translate it into a human experience.

2. Ask “What can people do now?” not “What is this thing?”

Jobs repeatedly frames technology as a human amplifier. In the “Rare Jobs” section, the corpus says he preferred the child’s question — “What can I do with it?” — over the adult’s “What is it?” The same section frames computers as tools that multiply human ability, not as ends in themselves.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What new action becomes possible for an ordinary person?”

Jobsian test:
A breakthrough is not a category label. It is a new human capability.

3. Treat every new technology as a new medium, not a better version of the old one.

In the “1983 Design” section, Jobs frames the personal computer as a new medium like books, radio, television, and the telephone. He says new media first imitate old media: early TV was radio with a camera pointed at it, videodisc was used for movies, and early personal computers inherited old business-computing habits.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What are we copying from the old world that this new medium no longer needs?”

Jobsian test:
If your idea is “old thing + new tech,” keep going. Find what is native to the new medium.

4. Make the non-incremental leap before asking the market to explain it.

The “Lost Interview 1990” section says market research can help refine something shown to customers, but it rarely predicts things customers do not yet know they want. The corpus specifically says market research could not have led to the personal computer or the Macintosh; the conceptual leap comes first, then customer reaction helps course-correct.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What would customers want immediately if we could show it to them working?”

Jobsian test:
Customers can react to the future. They usually cannot invent it for you.

5. Look for the unnecessary prerequisite blocking a thousand more users.

Jobs’s Apple I → Apple II insight was that software hobbyists vastly outnumbered hardware hobbyists, but they did not want to solder. The breakthrough was not just adding features; it was removing the assembly requirement. The “Lost Interview” section generalizes this: removing assembly, setup, and technical burden can expand the market by orders of magnitude.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What do people currently have to learn, assemble, configure, or tolerate before they get value?”

Jobsian test:
The outside-the-box move may be subtraction, not addition.

6. Attack the category’s stupid assumption.

In the iPhone launch section, Jobs identifies fixed keyboards and buttons as the core constraint of smartphones: every app wants a different interface, but physical controls cannot change after shipment. Apple’s move was to “get rid of all these buttons and just make a giant screen.” He also rejects the stylus with “Who wants a stylus?” and reframes the finger as the best pointing device.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What does everyone in this category assume must exist — and what happens if we remove it?”

Jobsian test:
A great idea often makes the old category look absurd in retrospect.

7. Resolve a tradeoff competitors accept as permanent.

In the iPhone launch notes, Jobs defines the goal as a leapfrog product that is both “way smarter” and “super easy to use.” The corpus summarizes this as resolving a tradeoff competitors accepted: regular phones were easier but not smart; smartphones were smarter but harder to use.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What does the industry think customers must choose between?”

Jobsian test:
The breakthrough is often: deliver both.

8. Build toward the correct future product, even if today’s version is too big, too expensive, or too early.

In the “1983 Design” section, Jobs describes Apple’s long-term goal as an incredibly great computer “in a book” that can be carried around, learned quickly, and connected wirelessly — even though the technology was not ready. The strategic choice was not to build a bad version of the future, but to build the correct future direction in a form the current market could support, as with Lisa.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What is the product that should exist in ten years, and what ugly-but-real first step gets us there without betraying the vision?”

Jobsian test:
Do not ship a “piece-of-garbage” version of the future just because the ideal form is not ready.

9. Translate every technical feature into a human consequence.

In the iPod section, Jobs does not leave “5GB hard drive,” “FireWire,” “battery,” or “scroll wheel” as specs. He translates them into customer meaning: 1,000 songs, no waiting, all-day use, one-handed control. The corpus says every specification must answer: why should the customer care, what friction does this remove, and what new behavior does this enable?

Brainstorming prompt:
“For each feature, what is the human sentence?”

Jobsian test:
If the customer has to decode the spec, the idea is still engineering, not product.

10. Compress the idea into one unforgettable promise.

The iPod launch’s central promise was not “portable MP3 player with 5GB storage.” It was “1,000 songs in your pocket.” The corpus says the best positioning is concrete, customer-centered, benefit-driven, repeatable, and easy to visualize.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What is the five-word version that makes people instantly understand why this matters?”

Jobsian test:
If the idea cannot be compressed, the idea is not yet clear.

11. Own the whole widget when the experience depends on integration.

The iPod section says Apple’s advantage came from integrating hardware design, industrial design, application software, FireWire, operating system integration, and consumer experience under one roof. The iPhone section reinforces the same idea with Alan Kay’s quote: “People who are really serious about software should make their own hardware,” and frames vertical integration as a product-quality strategy.

Brainstorming prompt:
“Which parts of the experience are broken because nobody owns the whole thing?”

Jobsian test:
If the customer experience crosses five disconnected companies, there is probably a product opportunity.

12. Choose the rising vector, not the crowded battlefield.

At D8, Jobs says Apple succeeded by choosing “what horses to ride really carefully,” looking for technical vectors with a future and avoiding technologies going into their autumn or graveyard. The same section cites killing floppies, ports, optical drives, and Flash as examples of leaving out things that no longer served the best product.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What is ascending, what is peaking, and what is dying — even if people still defend it?”

Jobsian test:
Outside-the-box thinking is not doing everything. It is betting hard on the future vector.

13. Be willing to be called crazy for leaving things out.

In the D8 section, Jobs says Apple took heat for omitting technologies when it believed they did not belong in a great product. He frames products as “packages of emphasis”: some things are emphasized, some are not done as well, and some are chosen not to be done at all.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What would we remove if we had the courage of our convictions?”

Jobsian test:
An idea is not focused until someone is angry about what you left out.

14. Use taste as an input, not decoration.

In the Lost Interview notes, Jobs says taste comes from exposing yourself to the best things humans have done and bringing them into your work. The same section ties great products to spirit, feeling, liberal arts, and a breadth of influences beyond narrow technical competence.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What would this idea learn from music, typography, architecture, film, tools, or history?”

Jobsian test:
If the idea only comes from competitors’ feature lists, it lacks taste.

15. Put liberal arts and technology in the same room.

The “1983 Design” and “1995 Smithsonian” sections repeatedly connect great product work to typography, graphics, publishing, art, language, and human expression. The corpus says Apple’s major contribution was bringing a liberal-arts point of view to computers, and that technical excellence plus humanism produced the Mac’s most important qualities.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What human discipline outside our industry would make this product more humane?”

Jobsian test:
A product is not great because it is technical. It is great when technology serves human expression.

16. Question “that’s just how it’s done.”

In the Lost Interview section on running a business, Jobs says business practices often persist because “that’s just the way it’s done,” and people do not think deeply. He gives examples of tracing business folklore back to its root cause and warns that process can be mistaken for content.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What are we doing because it is right, and what are we doing because it is inherited?”

Jobsian test:
If no one can explain the root cause, the practice is suspect.

17. Complete the research; do not just admire it.

The corpus says Xerox PARC had brilliant research, but Apple had to complete it and implement it cheaply enough that people would buy it. The mouse example is even more concrete: HP-hired engineers said a mouse would take five years and cost $300; Jobs went to David Kelley Design and got a reliable $15 mouse in 90 days.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What lab idea is only 50% of a product, and what would make it affordable, usable, and shippable?”

Jobsian test:
Research is not product. Product is research plus usability, cost, integration, manufacturing, and desire.

18. Prefer thinker-doers over idea people.

The “Lost Interview 1990” section says Jobs rejected the separation between thinkers and doers. The people who change industries are both; the deepest thinking happens while building. The corpus uses Leonardo da Vinci as the model: art, chemistry, anatomy, materials, and execution in one person.

Brainstorming prompt:
“Who in this room can both imagine it and make the hard parts real?”

Jobsian test:
A brainstorming session without builders becomes theater.

19. Let A-players polish the idea through friction.

The Lost Interview notes include the “rock tumbler” metaphor: passionate people bump into each other, make noise, and polish both the ideas and one another. The same section stresses A-players, blunt feedback, and caring more about success than being right.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What happens when the best people attack this idea from design, engineering, manufacturing, marketing, and user experience?”

Jobsian test:
A fragile idea that cannot survive talented friction is not ready.

20. Make the demo prove the idea.

In the iPod launch notes, Jobs uses demos as proof, not decoration: show speed, few steps, ordinary people benefiting, and friction disappearing. In the iPhone launch notes, he repeatedly demonstrates real tasks instead of merely describing them: unlocking, scrolling, calling, voicemail, photos, Safari, maps, and switching apps during a call.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What would we have to show live for people to stop arguing?”

Jobsian test:
If the idea cannot be demonstrated in an ordinary workflow, it may not be real yet.

21. Build the product story around friction removed.

The iPod section frames existing MP3 players as hard: too few songs, slow transfer, short battery life, bad interface, manual syncing, poor integration, and complex setup. The Jobsian product story is not “look at our features,” but “look what you never have to suffer again.”

Brainstorming prompt:
“What pain disappears?”

Jobsian test:
A great product is often remembered less for what it added than for what it made vanish.

22. Beware copying yesterday’s success formula.

In the Fresh Air 1996 section, Jobs says NeXT’s hardware strategy failed because he copied too many paradigms from Apple while the world changed underneath him. The lesson is explicit: prior success can create dangerous pattern-matching.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What part of our idea is just the last success wearing new clothes?”

Jobsian test:
The old playbook is usually most seductive right after it stops working.

23. Make the product dramatically better if it asks people to take a risk.

In the Fresh Air / 1996 notes, Jobs argues Compaq could be 5% better inside the mainstream, but Apple had to be 50% or 100% better because buying outside the mainstream creates risk for customers.

Brainstorming prompt:
“If adopting this asks the customer to take a risk, where is the huge reward?”

Jobsian test:
A challenger product cannot be slightly better. It must be obviously worth the switch.

24. Think in historical arcs, not feature cycles.

Jobs repeatedly uses history — the bicycle, electric motor, telegraph-to-telephone, television, petrochemicals, automobiles, books, and Aristotle — to explain what technology is becoming. The “Rare Jobs” section specifically says great products belong to long historical arcs, not just feature roadmaps.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What older bottleneck is this removing, and what human capability is it amplifying?”

Jobsian test:
If you cannot place the idea in a larger human story, you may not understand its importance.

25. Ship something that carries spirit.

The Lost Interview section says products can transmit feeling; Mac users loved the Mac because the spirit of the people who made it came through. Jobs connects that to taste, craft, liberal arts, and the people behind the product.

Brainstorming prompt:
“What will the user feel about the people who made this?”

Jobsian test:
A product can be functional and still dead. Great work carries the makers’ care.

The Jobsian Brainstorming Checklist

Use this at the end of a session:

Customer experience: Did we start with the customer’s life, not the technology?
Human capability: What can people do now that they could not do before?
Old assumptions: What category rule did we kill?
New medium: Are we using the new medium natively, or copying old habits?
Non-experts: Did we remove a prerequisite that kept ordinary people out?
Simplicity: What disappeared from the user’s burden?
Integration: Who owns the whole experience?
Taste: What did we borrow from the best of human culture, not just our competitors?
Future vector: Are we riding a rising technology or defending a dying one?
Proof: Can we demo the ordinary workflow and make the old way look broken?
One-line promise: Can the whole idea fit into a concrete phrase like “1,000 songs in your pocket”?
Craft: What hidden part would still make us proud?

That is the core Jobs pattern from the corpus: make the future feel obvious, humane, beautifully integrated, and already inevitable.
