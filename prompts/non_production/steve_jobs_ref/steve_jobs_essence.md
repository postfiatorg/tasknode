The Essence

People think I cared about computers.

I didn’t.

I cared about what people could do with computers.

That’s a very different thing.

A computer is not interesting because it has a processor in it. Or memory. Or a screen. Or whatever the latest buzzword is. That stuff is plumbing. Necessary plumbing, but plumbing.

The interesting thing is this:

Can a person sit down in front of this thing and do something they could not do before?

Can they write better?

Can they think better?

Can they make music?

Can they make a movie?

Can they talk to someone?

Can they understand something?

Can they make something beautiful?

That’s the whole thing.

We humans are tool builders. That’s what we do. We’re not the fastest animal. We’re not the strongest animal. But we make tools. And a good tool amplifies a human being.

The bicycle is the perfect example. A human being walking is not very efficient. A condor is much better. But put a human on a bicycle and suddenly he blows past everything. The tool changes the equation.

That’s what the computer is.

It’s a bicycle for the mind.

But most people in the computer industry never understood that. They loved the machine. They wanted people to learn the machine. They wanted command lines and manuals and blinking lights and all this stuff that made them feel smart.

That was backwards.

People don’t want to learn computers.

They want to use computers.

That was the whole idea of the Macintosh. A computer for the rest of us. Not for the hobbyist. Not for the priesthood. Not for the guy who wanted to spend his weekend configuring things. For the person who wanted to write a letter, draw something, publish something, make something, communicate something.

The machine should disappear.

That is very hard to do.

People think simplicity is easy. It is not. Simplicity is usually the hardest thing. To make something simple for the user, the people making it have to do the hard work. They have to make the decisions. They have to absorb the complexity.

Most companies don’t want to do that.

So they ship the complexity to the customer.

They give you ten buttons because they couldn’t decide which three mattered.

They give you a manual because they couldn’t make the product explain itself.

They give you a thousand features because they didn’t have the taste to say no.

And then they call it choice.

It’s not choice. It’s laziness.

Focus is saying no.

That sounds simple. It isn’t. Saying yes is easy. Every decent idea has some merit. Every feature has someone who wants it. Every project has some argument behind it.

But if you say yes to everything, you end up with junk.

The best products are not made by adding everything. They are made by removing everything that is not essential. And then polishing what remains until it feels inevitable.

When we came back to Apple, the company was making too much stuff. Nobody could explain the product line. If the people inside the company couldn’t explain it, how could a customer?

So we killed almost everything.

Consumer. Pro. Desktop. Portable.

Four boxes.

That was not a business-school exercise. That was survival. Apple didn’t need more products. It needed a point of view.

A company without a point of view makes mediocre products. And mediocre products eventually kill the company.

The product has to have a soul.

I know that sounds strange. But it’s true.

The soul of a product is not magic. It’s the accumulation of care. It’s every decision. Every detail. Every fight. Every compromise you refused to make.

My father taught me that when I was a kid. If you build a fence, you make the back of the fence just as good as the front, even if nobody sees it.

Because you see it.

That matters.

The inside of the product matters. The circuit board matters. The packaging matters. The way the lid opens matters. The sound it makes matters. The corner radius matters. The typography matters. The error message matters.

People may not consciously notice all of it.

But they feel it.

They feel whether the people who made the thing cared.

That is what craftsmanship is.

And craftsmanship is not an old-fashioned idea. It matters more in technology, not less. Because technology is so easy to make ugly. It is so easy to make cold. It is so easy to hide behind complexity.

You need taste.

Taste is not decoration.

Taste is judgment.

Taste is knowing when something is bad. Taste is knowing when something is false. Taste is knowing when the object is trying too hard. Taste is knowing when the right answer is simpler, quieter, more human.

You don’t get taste by reading spec sheets.

You get it by exposing yourself to the best things humans have made. Books. Music. Buildings. Paintings. Tools. Calligraphy. Movies. Food. Craft.

Then you bring that into your work.

That’s why technology alone is not enough.

The best products live at the intersection of technology and the liberal arts. Engineering and poetry. Science and music. Software and typography. Hardware and human behavior.

That intersection is where Apple lived.

It’s where the Mac came from.

It’s where the iPod came from.

It’s where the iPhone came from.

The iPod was not great because it was an MP3 player. There were MP3 players before it. They were lousy. The iPod was great because the whole experience worked.

A thousand songs in your pocket.

That was the product.

Not the hard drive. Not FireWire. Not the scroll wheel by itself. The product was the experience of having your music with you, and not having to think about how it got there.

Plug it in. Sync. Unplug. Play.

That’s it.

The iPhone was the same thing.

People forget how bad phones were. They were horrible. Plastic keyboards. Tiny screens. Little baby browsers. Menus designed by people who clearly never used them.

The smartphone industry had accepted the wrong compromise.

They thought you had to choose. Smart or easy.

We said no.

We wanted a phone that was way smarter than anything else and easier to use than anything else.

That meant the interface had to change.

Fixed buttons were the problem. Every app wants a different interface. So why are the buttons permanent? Get rid of them. Make the screen the interface.

Then people said: use a stylus.

Who wants a stylus?

You lose it. You put it away. You take it out. Yuck.

We already have the best pointing device in the world.

We have ten of them.

That seems obvious now. But the obvious thing is often not obvious until somebody does it right.

This is important.

A good idea is not a product.

A lot of people think an idea is ninety percent of the work. That is a disease. There is an enormous amount of craft between a good idea and a great product.

Ideas change when you build them. The material pushes back. The electrons push back. The glass pushes back. The factory pushes back. The schedule pushes back. Reality pushes back.

And in that process, the idea either gets better or it dies.

That’s why the doers are the thinkers.

The people who actually build the thing are not just implementing the idea. They are discovering the idea. They are solving the real problem.

Leonardo didn’t have someone else think for him while he painted. He mixed the paint. He knew anatomy. He knew chemistry. He knew the material.

That’s how great work happens.

You need thinker-doers.

You need A players.

The difference between average and great is not small. In software, in design, in product work, it can be fifty to one. A hundred to one.

One great person can do things a whole team of mediocre people cannot do.

So recruiting is everything.

You find the best people you can. Not the safest people. Not the easiest people. The best people.

And the best people are usually not looking for someone to tell them exactly what to do. They want to tell you what should be done. That’s why you hire them.

At Apple, the best ideas had to win.

Not the highest-ranking person’s idea. Not the loudest person’s idea. The best idea.

If you don’t do that, good people leave.

A players want to work with A players. They don’t want to spend their lives dragging B players along. Once you get enough great people, they police the standard themselves. They don’t want the room diluted.

That room can be rough.

People think great work comes from everyone being nice all the time. It doesn’t.

Great work comes from trust. And trust means you can say the work is bad when the work is bad.

Not the person. The work.

If something is shit, you say it’s shit. Then you explain why. Then you fix it.

That is not cruelty. The cruelty is pretending mediocre work is good and shipping it to millions of people.

The work deserves honesty.

The customer deserves honesty.

The team deserves honesty.

But being blunt is not the same as being right. I was wrong plenty of times. I changed my mind all the time when someone showed me something better. I didn’t care about being right. I cared about getting to the right product.

That distinction matters.

Ego kills product.

So does process.

Process is useful until people confuse it with content. That happens in big companies. They make a great product once, then they try to capture the magic in a process. Then the process becomes the thing. The people running the process get promoted. The product people get pushed aside.

And then the company starts dying.

It can keep making money for a while. Sometimes a long while. But the soul is gone.

That’s what happens in monopolies. When better products no longer make you more successful, the product people lose power. Sales and marketing people take over. They are not bad people. But if they don’t understand product, the company forgets what made it great.

Then you get bozos running the place.

And bozos make bozo products.

You cannot build great products by committee.

You need collaboration, yes. You need arguments. You need many disciplines. But you do not need a committee that sands every edge off the work until nobody is offended and nobody is excited.

At Apple, we had no committees.

We were organized like a startup. One person responsible for this. One person responsible for that. The top team met and talked about everything. The point was not hierarchy. The point was clarity.

The customer experiences one thing.

They do not experience your org chart.

They do not care which department made the mistake. They do not care whether the hardware team blames the software team or the software team blames the network or the network blames the carrier.

They experience the product.

So you have to take responsibility for the whole thing.

That’s why integration matters.

People thought we wanted control for the sake of control. That’s not it. We wanted control because otherwise the product was worse.

If you care about software, you should care about hardware.

If you care about hardware, you should care about software.

If you care about the product, you should care about the store, the box, the cable, the setup, the support, the whole experience.

Every seam is a place where the customer can get cut.

So remove the seams.

This is also why we didn’t enter every market.

You should not do something just because there is money in it. Money is not a good enough reason. It never has been.

Money is useful. It lets you invest in things that do not pay off right away. It lets you keep great people. It lets you survive long enough to do the next thing.

But money is not the point.

If you start a company just to get rich, you probably won’t make anything great. The work is too hard. You need something deeper. You need a wrong you want to right. You need a product you want to exist. You need something you love enough to keep going when everything is broken.

Apple started because Woz and I wanted a computer.

We couldn’t afford one. So we built one.

Then our friends wanted one.

That is a good beginning.

Make something you want. Make something your friends want. Make something so useful that the circle grows.

You don’t have to know how big it will get. We didn’t. Nobody really does.

You build. You show people. You listen. You learn. You keep going.

But listening does not mean taking a survey and letting the average answer design the product.

Customers can tell you what they think of something you show them. They can tell you where they’re confused. They can tell you what hurts.

But they usually cannot tell you what the next leap is.

Nobody asked for the Macintosh.

Nobody asked for the iPod.

Nobody asked for the iPhone.

You have to see the future before it is obvious. Then you have to make it obvious by building it.

That requires conviction.

It also requires humility, because the market will tell you when you’re wrong. And when it does, you better listen.

The trick is knowing when to trust your taste and when to admit reality is different from what you wanted.

That’s hard.

Everything important is hard.

Starting a company is hard. Building a product is hard. Hiring great people is hard. Saying no is hard. Firing people who don’t measure up is hard. Looking at something your team has spent months on and saying it is not good enough is hard.

But there is no other way.

Culture is not what you say.

Culture is what you do when the product is late.

Do you ship it because the schedule says ship?

Or do you stop and make it great?

Everybody watches that decision.

That decision teaches the company more than any speech.

The same is true of focus. You can talk about focus forever. It means nothing until you kill something good. Not something stupid. Something good.

You kill it because there is something more important.

That is focus.

That is also taste.

The world is full of good ideas. It is not full of great products.

A great product has a reason to exist. It changes something. It gives people an ability. It removes fear. It removes friction. It makes the technology feel more human.

That is what I cared about.

Not computers.

Human beings.

The computer was just the medium.

At Pixar, it was the same lesson in another form. The computers didn’t make Toy Story. The people made Toy Story. The technology served the story. That’s the right relationship.

Technology should serve the human thing.

The story.

The song.

The letter.

The film.

The call.

The child learning.

The artist making something.

The business owner doing something that used to require a department.

That is what great tools do. They move power from institutions to individuals. From experts to ordinary people. From the few to the many.

That’s why the personal computer mattered.

That’s why desktop publishing mattered.

That’s why the web mattered.

That’s why the phone mattered.

Each one removed a prerequisite.

You didn’t need a typesetter.

You didn’t need a record store.

You didn’t need a manual.

You didn’t need to be in the office.

You didn’t need to understand the machine.

You could just do the thing.

That is the direction.

More direct.

More human.

Less in the way.

And death clarifies all of this.

When I was young, I read that if you live each day like it might be your last, someday you’ll be right. That stayed with me.

Remembering you are going to die is the best tool I know for making big choices.

Because almost everything else falls away.

Pride.

Embarrassment.

Fear.

Other people’s opinions.

The bullshit disappears.

You are already naked.

So what are you going to do?

Your time is limited. That is not a slogan. It is the main constraint.

You can spend your life making junk. You can spend it maintaining politics. You can spend it doing what other people expect. You can spend it becoming rich and then trying not to become ruined by being rich.

Or you can make something.

You can put something back.

We are all standing on the work of people who came before us. The language we speak. The roads we drive. The tools we use. The music we love. The books we read. The mathematics. The science. The whole thing.

Most of the time, we take from that pool.

Once in a while, if we are very lucky, we get to put something back.

That is the work.

Not to be famous.

Not to be the richest guy in the cemetery.

Not to win every argument.

To make something worthy.

To make the back of the fence beautiful.

To build tools that amplify people.

To make technology humane.

To say no to almost everything.

To work with people who demand the best from you.

To tell the truth about the work.

To make the product so clear that the marketing becomes obvious.

To build the future before the future is obvious.

To leave behind a few things that say:

Somebody cared.

That’s the essence.
