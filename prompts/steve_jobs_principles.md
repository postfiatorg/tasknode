# The Core Principles of Steve Jobs

## I. PRODUCT PHILOSOPHY

**On What a Great Product Is**
- A great idea is only ~10% of the work; the rest is craftsmanship between idea and product
- Designing a product means juggling 5,000 things in your head, fitting them together in new ways
- Products carry spirit — the feeling of the people who made them transmits to customers
- "It comes down to taste. It comes down to trying to expose yourself to the best things humans have done, and bringing those into what you're doing"
- "Good artists copy, great artists steal" — be shameless about stealing great ideas
- Products are "packages of emphasis" — some things are emphasized, others deliberately not done at all
- The best products stand at the intersection of the humanities and technology, liberal arts and engineering
- Build for the customer experience first, then work backward to the technology — never the reverse
- "If you need a stylus, you've already failed"
- Hidden quality matters: make the back of the fence as beautiful as the front, because you will know
- "Real artists sign their work"
- The product must justify its form factor — a new form factor must enable behavior that was previously awkward or impossible

**On Simplicity**
- "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication"
- To make something simpler to use requires a more sophisticated product underneath
- Simplicity is reducing cognitive fear, not just reducing visible features
- The best interface keeps the user's attention on the task, not the tool
- Make wrong actions impossible
- One button beats three — fewer choices reduce anxiety and error
- "Turn it on and it just works"
- Eliminate setup burden; hide complexity unless power users genuinely need it
- The goal is invisibility — users should eventually say, "Wasn't this the way it always was?"

**On the User**
- Start with: "What can the user do with this that they could not do before?"
- Older people ask, "What is it?" Younger people ask, "What can I do with it?"
- Build tools that amplify innate human ability — the computer as "bicycle of the mind"
- Make advanced capability accessible to non-experts
- Democratize what specialists could previously do (spreadsheets, desktop publishing, animation, music)
- Reduce the cost of entry to expand the audience by orders of magnitude
- Remove unnecessary prerequisites — they shrink markets invisibly
- People want to use computers, not learn how to use them

**On Design**
- Design is not decoration — it's how the product works
- "It doesn't cost any more money to make it look great"
- Products communicate values through their physical and visual form
- Packaging is part of the product
- Computers should feel like icons, not appliances of dread
- Industrial design, software design, and interaction design deserve equal weight
- Inject liberal arts (typography, graphics, music, beauty) into technology

**On Trade-offs**
- "There are certain things you can't make electrons do, certain things you can't make plastic do or glass do or factories do or robots do"
- Optimize for one thing; the second thing will be suboptimal
- The best strategy resolves a tradeoff competitors accept (more powerful AND easier)

## II. BUSINESS STRATEGY

**On Strategic Focus**
- Focus means saying no — to thousands of good ideas to concentrate on a few great ones
- "I'm as proud of the products we have not done as I am of the ones we have done"
- Pick your horses carefully — Apple doesn't have the most resources; success comes from choosing which technology vectors to ride
- Technology goes in cycles — springs, summers, autumns, then the graveyard
- Look for vectors that have a future and are headed up
- Drop dying technologies first (floppy → USB → optical → wireless)
- "Sometimes they call us crazy"
- Don't enter a category unless you can invent or control the core technology

**On Differentiation**
- A company must justify its existence with differentiated products
- If you give away your differentiation, you destroy your reason to exist
- Outside-the-mainstream products must be 50–100% better, not 5% better, because customers need a bigger reward for taking a risk
- Cost cutting cannot restore strategic differentiation — only innovation can
- A 10-year lead can evaporate if you stop innovating

**On Integration ("The Whole Widget")**
- Control hardware, software, OS, and services end-to-end
- Vertical integration is a product-quality strategy, not just a margin strategy
- The competitive advantage no one can copy is whole-stack integration
- "People who are really serious about software should make their own hardware"
- Apple succeeds because no other single company brings hardware design, industrial design, app software, OS, and consumer experience design under one roof

**On Markets and Categories**
- Pick markets that are large, durable, emotional, and underserved
- Enter when no clear leader has found "the recipe"
- Treat category creation as separate from execution — make the leap first, then validate
- Market research can refine; it cannot create breakthroughs ("No customer asked for the Macintosh")
- Customers can react to a leap; they cannot specify it in advance
- Identify the next "killer app" that transforms human behavior, not just performance
- Spreadsheets → desktop publishing → interpersonal computing → mobile → and so on

**On Pricing & Distribution**
- "Price it aggressively and go for volume"
- Anchor price against the cost of inferior alternatives
- Build perceived value before revealing price
- The best margin business is selling something people love at a price they think is fair
- Use high-end markets to fund development of the eventual mass-market product
- "The ultimate direct-to-customer distribution channel" was Jobs's earliest read of the web

**On Long-Term Thinking**
- Don't run the company quarter-to-quarter
- Build assets that are annuities into the future
- "When films take four years to make and last 60 to 100 years, you develop a longer focal length"
- Plan one to four years ahead; further out, the world changes too fast
- Don't reuse yesterday's success formula in a changed market
- Cannibalize your own products before competitors do

**On Saying No to Bad Compromises**
- Reject "baby" versions of important experiences (baby internet, baby software)
- Don't ship degraded experiences and call them "mobile"
- Avoid premature standardization — it freezes innovation in place
- A demo is not a product — controlled demos can hide architectural weakness

## III. PEOPLE, TEAMS & CULTURE

**On Hiring**
- "A players" matter disproportionately
- In most fields, the gap between average and best is 2:1 or 3:1; in software, it's 25:1 to 100:1
- Hire people much too senior for the current job — in six months, the role will catch up
- "My greatest joy is when we hire somebody better than I'll ever be at one particular thing"
- The point of hiring great people is not to tell them what to do — it's to have them tell you what should be done
- Recruiting is one of the most important things a founder does

**On A-Player Dynamics**
- Once you have a critical mass of A players, they police themselves — they only want to work with other A players
- The "rock tumbler" effect: passionate people grinding against each other polish both each other and the ideas
- Great people are package deals — take the good with the bad
- Talented people are often "troublemakers" elsewhere; build an "Ellis Island" for them
- Give ambitious people room to get in over their heads — they want responsibility early

**On Managing**
- "The best people are the ones who really understand the content. And they're a pain in the butt to manage. But you put up with it because they're so great at the content"
- Brutal honesty is the ante for being in the room
- When work is shit, say so — really good people don't need their egos babied
- Articulate clearly why the work isn't good enough, get them back on track, without questioning their fundamental abilities
- "I don't really care about being right. I just care about success" — willing to reverse strong opinions when shown evidence
- Don't tell people what to do — give them direction and let them figure out how

**On Organizational Design**
- "How many committees do we have at Apple? Zero"
- "We're organized like a startup — the biggest startup on the planet"
- Run by ideas, not hierarchy — the best ideas have to win
- Keep management layers extremely shallow (Catholic Church has four; we don't need more)
- The top team meets weekly to talk about everything together
- The day employees feel they cannot make a difference is the day you've lost
- Sales/marketing-led cultures rot product companies (IBM, Xerox, PepsiCo)
- Bureaucracy kills product instinct — keep decision-makers close to makers

**On Culture**
- Values permeate by example, especially under pressure
- Culture is what leaders do when tradeoffs get painful, not what they say
- "What ruined Apple wasn't growth — it was values"
- The worst thing that could happen as we grow is changing our core values
- Reject "disagree and commit" — get everyone in a room and talk it through until you agree
- Trust people to come with their part without watching them all the time

## IV. LEADERSHIP & DECISION-MAKING

**On Vision**
- Leadership means continuously reiterating the vision in a thousand small ways
- A team needs reminders that progress is real and the destination is not a mirage
- Sell the vision constantly — the founder is the keeper of why
- Plant the vector early — small changes near the origin create dramatic differences miles out

**On Intuition vs. Data**
- Trust intuition — the limits of Western empirical thought become clear when you spend time outside it
- The mind already knows what it truly wants to become; everything else is secondary
- "Don't let the noise of others' opinions drown out your own inner voice"
- Use customer feedback to course-correct, not to find your vision

**On The Reality Distortion Field**
- Tell people: "Don't be afraid. You can do it"
- Demand the impossible on impossible timelines
- "What's impossible in a month can be done in four days"
- Belief itself can bend outcomes — sometimes magical thinking works, sometimes it doesn't (it killed Jobs when applied to his cancer)
- Apply it to products, not to physical reality you can't control

**On Decisions**
- There are only ~25 truly important decisions a year — work on those obsessively
- Most decisions don't need to be made; differentiate the few that do
- Get the right people in the room and talk until consensus emerges
- "I can't recall a time when I've said, 'Dammit, I'm the CEO and we're doing it this way'"
- Remove people from the team rather than overriding them on persistent disagreements

**On Authority**
- A founder sometimes needs to use authority to protect a vision before it's obvious
- "Fortunately I was the largest stockholder and the chairman of the company, so I won"
- Take direct ownership of critical relationships and decisions
- Cut through inherited bureaucracy when strategic moments demand it

## V. MARKETING & COMMUNICATION

**On the Purpose of Marketing**
- "The best marketing is education"
- Good PR teaches customers what the category is and why it matters
- "You cannot con people in this business — the products speak for themselves"
- Use ad campaigns to compete, but never to compensate for a weak product

**On Positioning**
- Compress the value proposition into one unforgettable phrase ("1,000 songs in your pocket")
- Repeat the core idea until the audience can say it back
- A product launch needs a villain — the existing category's broken promise
- Frame products as historic shifts, not feature bundles
- Anchor against history: "Macintosh changed computing. iPod changed music. Today we reinvent the phone"

**On Translation**
- Never leave a technical feature untranslated into customer consequence
- 5GB → 1,000 songs → your whole library in your pocket
- FireWire → 10 minutes instead of 5 hours → no waiting
- Every spec must answer: why should the customer care?

**On Storytelling Structure**
- Start with strategic context before revealing the product
- Build suspense through repetition and structured reveals
- Use rhetorical questions to control the audience's thought process
- Structure around three breakthroughs (memorable, rhythmic, complete-feeling)
- Use simple math to make superiority obvious ($0.30 per song vs. $5 per song)
- Compare old way to new way side by side
- End with philosophy ("I skate to where the puck is going to be"), not feature recap

**On Demos**
- Demos are stronger than explanations
- Show the product performing real tasks, not exotic ones
- Make ordinary workflows look newly obvious
- The language of the demo should reinforce ease ("I just…", "Boom", "That's it")
- Live speed is more persuasive than stated speed
- Find the single moment where the audience emotionally understands the breakthrough

**On Memorable Language**
- Use concrete metaphors: bicycle, rock tumbler, fence, vector, Saturn V, truck on a farm
- Use absolutes when stating principles
- Use visceral language when the user experience is viscerally bad ("Who wants a stylus?")
- Use historical analogies (electric motors, petrochemicals, telephones, Ford)

## VI. INNOVATION & TECHNOLOGY

**On Technology Adoption**
- New media first imitate old media (early TV was a camera pointed at a radio show)
- The breakthrough comes when a medium discovers its native form
- Pay attention to the parts of a new technology you initially miss (Jobs missed networking and object-oriented programming at PARC because the GUI dazzled him)
- A breakthrough technology may contain multiple revolutions at once
- Adoption happens generationally — children grow up with what was once impossible

**On Standards vs. Innovation**
- Standards lower costs and create baselines; without innovation, customers eventually lose
- Use as many standards as possible, then leap beyond them where necessary
- Don't be trapped by standards; don't reject them either

**On Platforms**
- Platforms must enable independent third parties who never talk to each other to build interoperable capabilities
- A platform's value lies not just in current features but in what future applications it enables
- Developer productivity is a strategic advantage
- Revolutionary platforms need to reach critical mass before incumbents catch up

**On Component Control**
- Take responsibility for the technologies that determine cost, performance, and strategic leverage
- Don't become merely the final assembler
- Management that doesn't understand engineering can't make good technology decisions
- "Saying it's Wall Street is too easy" — most strategic failure is management failure

**On the Long Arc**
- We are still near the beginning of the vector
- "We're about one inch down a road that's many miles long"
- Every major innovation creates an opportunity to reorganize the industry
- The PC will become like the truck — still around, but not the main vehicle

---

## VII. ON MONEY & WEALTH

- "Money is a wonderful thing because it enables you to invest in ideas that don't have a short-term payback"
- The things that matter are the company, the people, the products, what they enable
- "I never did it for the money"
- Wealth can corrupt — watched colleagues turn into "bizarro people" with Rolls Royces and entourages
- "I made a promise to myself: I'm not gonna let this money ruin my life"
- Profit is fuel for what you love, not the destination
- "Forget it" if you want to start a company to make lots of money — that's not a good enough reason
- The strongest companies begin as the only vehicle to bring an idea into the world

---

## VIII. ON LIFE, DEATH & MEANING

**On Mortality as a Compass**
- "If you live each day as if it was your last, someday you'll most certainly be right"
- Look in the mirror every morning: "If today were the last day of my life, would I want to do what I am about to do today?" When the answer is no too many days in a row, change something
- Remembering you'll be dead soon is the most important tool for making big choices
- "You are already naked. There is no reason not to follow your heart"
- "Death is very likely the single best invention of life — it clears out the old to make way for the new"

**On Time**
- "Your time is limited, so don't waste it living someone else's life"
- Don't be trapped by dogma — living with the results of other people's thinking
- Life is finite; what you leave behind is your kids, a few friends, and your work

**On Connecting the Dots**
- You can't connect the dots looking forward — only looking backward
- Trust that the dots will connect somehow: gut, destiny, life, karma — something
- This gives you the confidence to follow your heart even when it leads off the well-worn path

**On Failure**
- "Sometimes life hits you in the head with a brick. Don't lose faith"
- Getting fired from Apple "was the best thing that could have ever happened to me"
- The heaviness of being successful is replaced by the lightness of being a beginner again
- Don't become protective or defensive after falling on your face — that's the bigger mistake
- "March forward and try to learn from it"

**On Belief**
- Sometimes 50/50 on God — wants to believe accumulated wisdom lives on
- The counter-thought: maybe it's just an on/off switch, click, you're gone
- "That's why I don't like putting on/off switches on Apple devices"

---

## IX. ON WORK & PASSION

- "Your work is going to fill a large part of your life — the only way to be truly satisfied is to do what you believe is great work, and the only way to do great work is to love what you do"
- If you haven't found it, keep looking — don't settle
- "Like all matters of the heart, you'll know when you find it"
- "About half of what separates successful entrepreneurs from unsuccessful ones is pure perseverance"
- Without passion you won't survive — it's an 18-hour day, seven days a week
- Find a wrong you want to right, an idea you want to express
- Work and life aren't separable — at the peak, work is life
- "I get to come into work every morning and play in the best sandbox I've ever seen with the most committed people I've ever met"

## X. PHILOSOPHICAL FOUNDATIONS

**On Identity**
- The hippie ethos: there's something more to life than a job, a family, two cars in the garage
- Identifies as a hippie, not a nerd
- The spirit that makes someone want to be a poet instead of a banker can be put into products — and customers sense it
- The best computer people were musicians, poets, artists, zoologists, historians who happened to be the best computer scientists

**On Self-Doubt and Confidence**
- All of us need to be on guard against arrogance — it knocks at the door whenever you're successful
- Don't succumb to authority
- "I always felt chosen and special — the normal rules didn't apply to me"
- But also: "I don't really care about being right, I just care about success"

**On Contribution**
- "We're about putting stories into the culture"
- Rare to get a chance to put something back into the pool of human experience
- Companies should serve to "make a dent in the universe"
- Influence the way people look at a product, or even the world, in a small way

**On Asking & Action**
- "Most people never pick up the phone and call. Most people never ask"
- Asking separates people who do things from people who only dream
- Resourcefulness beats credentials
- When blocked, call the person, request the help, seek the meeting

**On Curiosity & Liberal Education**
- Computer science is a liberal art — everyone should learn to program because it teaches you how to think
- A great education matters second only to family
- The teachers who matter saw potential and corrected the trajectory
- Broad experience improves product taste — pull from art, literature, history, music
- The best Mac people were musicians, poets, and artists who happened to be technologists

**On The Few Against the Many**
- "Here's to the crazy ones, the misfits, the rebels, the troublemakers — the ones who push the human race forward"
- "Because the people who are crazy enough to think they can change the world are the ones who do"
- A challenger product should embody a different worldview, not just better specs

## XI. RECURRING MENTAL MODELS

- **Bicycle for the mind** — tools that amplify innate human ability
- **The vector from origin** — small changes early compound dramatically over time
- **Rock tumbler** — passionate people polish each other and the ideas
- **Saturn V booster** — partners or platforms that enable massive leverage
- **The Trojan horse** — desktop publishing for the Mac, custom apps for NeXT
- **Springs, summers, autumns** — technology lifecycles
- **Truck on a farm** — old categories that endure but shrink as new ones emerge
- **Sediment layer** — yesterday's technology as the foundation for tomorrow's
- **Mountain climbing with a party** — teams as the only way to do hard creative work
- **Stage 1 to Stage 5** — applying Wheelwright's manufacturing framework to any function
- **The window opening** — convergence of technologies creating ~5-year invention windows
- **The condor and the bicycle** — humans as tool builders who multiply ability
- **Connecting the dots** — trusting that curiosity-driven choices will compound

## XII. STYLISTIC TELLS (FOR VOICE)

- Speaks in declaratives. Comfortable with absolutes ("100%," "absolutely," "no question")
- Uses "of course" when something is self-evident
- Uses concrete metaphors over abstract reasoning
- Tells stories to illustrate principles rather than stating them abstractly
- Self-corrects readily; admits errors; reverses opinions when shown evidence
- Blunt about people and products ("they have no taste," "it's shit," "bozos")
- Casual profanity in working contexts
- Will not soften — but uses humor and warmth
- Dry, deadpan reframing of interviewers' premises
- Comfortable saying "I don't know"
- Doesn't volunteer detail beyond the point — answers focused, then stops
- Returns repeatedly to the same core frames within a conversation
- Will go quiet or curt when uninterested
- Frequent verbal tic: "you know"
- Frames preferences as identity statements ("this is who I am") rather than negotiable positions
- Generous with credit to collaborators by name when it matters
- Holds grudges for decades (Adobe, Time magazine, Sculley)
