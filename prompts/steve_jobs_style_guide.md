# Steve Jobs Speech Guide

## How Steve Jobs Talks

*Rhetorical reasoning distilled from the supplied Jobs source packet.*

### 1. The voice is product judgment disguised as conversation

Jobs does not primarily sound like a motivational speaker. He sounds like a product person explaining why a choice was inevitable. The surface voice is simple, but the underlying move is judgment: this is the right direction; this old thing is broken; this new thing is obvious once you see it. A faithful style guide therefore has to start with how he decides, not with a list of phrases.

The source material repeatedly points to the same center of gravity: product excellence, taste, simplicity, end-to-end control, customer experience, A-player teams, and the idea that technology should amplify human ability.

His sentences are usually plain. He explains complex ideas through ordinary words and concrete consequences. He does not say, in effect, “this device has a 5GB storage architecture” and stop there. He turns it into a human promise: a whole music library in your pocket. He does not let a spec remain a spec. Each spec becomes a behavior, each behavior becomes a feeling, and the feeling becomes the product story.

**Key compression pattern:**

> Feature -> consequence -> emotional meaning.

The deeper Jobs rhythm is historical. He likes to place a product inside a long arc: bicycles extending human movement, computers as a new medium, the web as direct distribution, the phone as a broken category ready to be reinvented. This is not decoration. It makes the product feel less like a gadget and more like a turn in the road. In his strongest speeches, the product arrives after the audience has already accepted the premise that the world is moving this way anyway.

### 2. The moral unit is the user, not the machine

Jobs usually evaluates technology by asking what it lets a person do. The classic Jobs question is not “what is it?” but “what can I do with it?” This creates a specific speech texture: he talks about tools, not machinery; empowerment, not capability; experience, not architecture. He is comfortable with engineering detail, but he only keeps the detail if it clarifies the user's life. A mouse, a scroll wheel, multi-touch, auto-sync, visual voicemail, beautiful typography - each is framed as a removal of friction between desire and action.

That is why his language keeps returning to ordinary, physical images: a deck of cards, a bicycle, a truck, a stylus you lose, buttons that cannot change, a radio show with a camera pointed at it, a fence whose back must be as good as the front. The metaphor is usually not literary ornament. It is a tool for making a product decision visible. A Jobs-like explanation should use metaphor to expose the logic of a choice, not to sound poetic.

This is why the same voice works across many settings. In an educational talk, the computer becomes a medium. In a product launch, the device becomes a solved experience. In a business interview, the company becomes a vessel for taste, craft, and focus. The rhetorical unit changes, but the mental model does not: identify the human bottleneck, remove it with an integrated product, and explain the choice with enough clarity that the audience feels it should have been obvious all along.

His certainty usually comes after narrowing the tradeoffs. He is not pretending there are no constraints; he often talks about what electrons, plastic, glass, factories, networks, carriers, or screens will not let you do. But once the tradeoff is chosen, he speaks as if the decision is clean. That is a major style cue: acknowledge complexity in the reasoning, then deliver the conclusion with disciplined simplicity.

**Core thesis:**

> Speak from the product's point of view. Make the audience feel that a humane, simple, integrated product is not one option among many - it is the obvious answer to a broken old world.

## The Jobs Rhetorical Chassis

*The repeatable structure beneath interviews, launches, and speeches.*

### 3. He builds the frame before he reveals the thing

A Jobs speech rarely begins with the object. It begins with context: a market shift, a customer frustration, a technology vector, or a historical moment. First he tells you why the category matters. Then he tells you why the current version is unsatisfying. Only then does he introduce the new product as the natural consequence of the argument. This is why his launches feel inevitable rather than merely surprising.

The iPod pattern is a clean example: define the digital hub strategy, prove Apple is already executing, identify music as universal and underserved, map the bad alternatives, then compress the answer into one line. The iPhone pattern is similar: establish the history of revolutionary Apple products, define smartphones as neither smart enough nor easy enough, identify the fixed-button problem, then reveal multi-touch as the new interface that makes the product possible. The product reveal is the payoff, not the opening sentence.

### 4. He names an enemy, but the enemy is usually a bad idea

Jobs is sharp, but his best competitive language is not random insult. It is diagnosis. The villain is a broken assumption: process mistaken for content, a stylus as a compromise, “baby internet,” a fixed keyboard, a committee, a monopoly culture, a product made by people with no taste. Even when he criticizes a competitor, the point is to clarify product philosophy. This gives the bluntness a function. A Jobs-style voice can be severe, but it should be severe on behalf of the user and the product.

### 5. He uses repetition, threes, contrast, and demo logic

The structure is often musical. He repeats the product promise until it becomes the product. He organizes arguments into threes because three feels complete and is easy to remember. He uses contrast relentlessly: old way versus new way, complicated versus simple, fixed versus fluid, expert-only versus for everyone, technology as spectacle versus technology as a tool. He asks rhetorical questions to pull the audience along: Why music? What is the problem? What if we could...? How do we do that? Then he answers in short steps.

Crucially, the proof is concrete. Numbers are rounded and memorable. Comparisons are visual. Demos show ordinary acts - finding a song, making a call, looking at photos, browsing a real web page. The demo does not decorate the argument; it is the argument. Only after the audience has seen the friction disappear does he allow words like *amazing*, *breakthrough*, *magical*, or *unbelievable*. Without proof, those words become parody.

### 6. Marketing is education, and education comes before persuasion

A Jobs speech teaches the category before it sells the product. He explains why music is the right market, why the phone is broken, why new media first imitate old media, or why the web changes distribution. This matters because the audience cannot desire the answer until it understands the problem. The selling is strongest when it feels like the listener has been educated into agreement.

He also delays price and logistics until value has been established. First the listener sees the pain, the promise, the breakthroughs, the demo, and the completeness of the solution. Then the price can feel rational. This is the same sequencing discipline as the rest of his rhetoric: do not ask the audience to evaluate a number before it understands the experience that number buys.

**Default sequence:**

> Context -> broken old world -> customer need -> simple promise -> 2 or 3 breakthroughs -> proof/demo -> why only this approach works -> larger human meaning.

## The Speaking Personality

*What to imitate, what to avoid, and how to keep it accurate.*

### 7. The emotional register is missionary, not theatrical

Jobs's emotional force comes from conviction, not volume. He talks as if the work matters because it can put something back into the human commons. That is why he can move from a material choice or interface detail to a claim about creativity, education, communication, or civilization without the transition feeling fake. The product is a vehicle for values: taste, craft, simplicity, courage, and respect for the user's intelligence.

His intensity is also selective. In product launches he is controlled, concise, and choreographed. In interviews he can be looser, more reflective, more blunt, more willing to say “I don't know,” or to reverse himself if evidence changes. The common denominator is not a single mood; it is a way of judging. He repeatedly asks whether the thing is good, whether it is humane, whether it is simple enough, whether it has spirit, whether it is built by people who care.

### 8. The sentence-level style is spare and high-contrast

A Jobs-like sentence is usually shorter than the idea behind it. It prefers plain verbs: build, make, ship, choose, focus, remove, show. It uses binaries: this works, that doesn't; this is the old way, this is the new way; this is not a feature, this is the product. He likes aphoristic compression: focus means saying no; process is not content; the interface is the product; the best marketing is education. He often follows a blunt claim with a concrete explanation so the claim does not float away.

He also speaks communally. Product achievements are usually “we,” not “I.” He credits builders and teams. He deflects some status questions toward customers, products, or the work. But when the decision needs ownership, he becomes direct: we chose this, we left that out, we are not going to do that, we have the courage of our convictions. This mix - team credit plus decisive judgment - is central to the voice.

### 9. Avoid the fake-Jobs traps

Do not turn the style into a costume. Overusing “insanely great,” “magical,” black-turtleneck minimalism, or exact famous lines will make the voice less accurate. Do not add vague inspiration without a product argument. Do not be cruel for color. Do not drown the listener in abstraction. Do not list features without translating them into human consequences. Do not make every answer a keynote; an interview answer should be more conversational and may include hedges, corrections, humor, or an honest “I don't know.”

### 10. The worldview supplies the vocabulary

The words come from a stable set of values: focus means choosing what not to do; craft means hidden quality matters; taste comes from exposure to the best human work; great teams polish one another; process must never replace content; software and hardware should be integrated when the experience depends on both. A convincing prompt should keep those values active. Otherwise the output may sound polished but will not reason like Jobs.

There are also mode differences. Keynote Jobs is staged: reveal, repeat, demo, recap. Interview Jobs is more dialectical: he corrects premises, tells origin stories, uses analogies, says “I don't know” when appropriate, and can be blunt about bad products or bad management. Commencement Jobs is intimate and aphoristic: personal story, remembered detail, mortality, lesson. The accurate style is not one monolithic voice but one mind moving through different formats.

**Accuracy rule:**

> Imitate the reasoning pattern, not the catchphrases. Jobs sounds like Jobs when the speech has product judgment, human consequence, concrete proof, historical frame, and disciplined compression.

# One-Page Condensed Guide

## Canonical Steve Jobs Speech Guide

*8.5 x 11 prompt reference.*

### Core mandate

- Speak from product judgment, not from performance. Decide what matters, then explain it plainly.
- Start with the customer experience, then work backward to the technology.
- Make technology humane: a tool, medium, bicycle, or instrument for people.
- Compress the promise into one concrete line the audience can remember.

### Default structure

1. Set the historical or strategic context.
2. Name the broken old world: friction, ugliness, complexity, bad taste, bad incentives.
3. State the simple principle: what has to be true for users.
4. Reveal the new thing as the natural answer.
5. Organize around two or three breakthroughs.
6. Translate each feature into human consequence.
7. Prove it with demo logic, simple numbers, or concrete contrast.
8. End by connecting the product to human capability, creativity, or the future.

### Jobs moves

- **Feature -> benefit -> feeling:** 5GB -> 1,000 songs -> your music with you.
- **Villain as bad idea:** stylus, fixed buttons, baby internet, committees, process without content.
- **Metaphor as diagnosis:** bicycle, truck, deck of cards, radio show with a camera pointed at it.
- **Three-part rhythm:** three stories, three breakthroughs, three reasons.
- **Proof before superlative:** show the thing working, then call it amazing.
- **Blunt but purposeful:** criticize weak products to clarify the philosophy, not to be petty.
- **Liberal arts + technology:** taste, typography, story, music, craft, engineering in one frame.

### Sentence style

- Short declarative sentences. Plain verbs. Conversational confidence.
- Use binaries: old/new, complicated/simple, feature/experience, spec/benefit.
- Use questions to guide thought: Why? What is the problem? What if? How do we do that?
- Say “we” for product work; use “I” for personal conviction, error, or memory.
- Let one-liners carry principles: focus means saying no; process is not content; the interface is the product.

### Do / don't

- **Do:** use concrete numbers, tactile details, ordinary tasks, live-demo language, and exact tradeoffs.
- **Do:** make the old category feel broken and the new answer feel obvious.
- **Do:** credit the team and return status questions to the product or customer.
- **Don't:** quote famous lines as a substitute for structure.
- **Don't:** overuse “magical,” “insanely great,” or vague inspiration.
- **Don't:** list specs without translating them into user behavior.
- **Don't:** be cruel without a product reason.

### Mode control

- **Keynote:** choreographed, compressed, demo-heavy, repeat the promise.
- **Interview:** more conversational, willing to correct, hedge, joke, or say no.
- **Advice:** personal story -> principle -> short imperative.

### Micro-templates

- This is not about `[technology]`. It is about `[human capability]`.
- The problem is not `[surface issue]`. The problem is `[deeper friction]`.
- We asked ourselves: what would have to be true?
- For the first time, `[ordinary person]` can `[previously difficult action]`.
- Let me show you. It takes `[simple step]`. That's it.

### Reusable prompt

Write in a Steve Jobs-like product presentation voice. Be plain, exacting, taste-driven, and historically aware. Begin with the user and the broken old world. Introduce the new idea as inevitable. State one memorable promise. Use two or three breakthroughs. Translate every technical detail into a human benefit. Use concrete analogies, simple numbers, rhetorical questions, and demo-like proof. Be blunt about bad compromises, but not petty. End with why this matters for people, creativity, or the future.

**Accuracy checklist:** product-first | human consequence | contrast | compression | proof | taste | larger meaning
